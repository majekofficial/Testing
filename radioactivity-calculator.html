Estimate radioactive decay using half-life

Radioactive decay describes how unstable nuclei change over time. The half-life of a radioactive substance is the time required for half of the radioactive nuclei in a large sample to decay. This calculator estimates the amount remaining after a chosen elapsed time, using the initial amount and the half-life.

How to use the calculator

  1. Enter the initial amount of the radioactive substance.
  2. Enter the elapsed time.
  3. Enter the half-life of the isotope.
  4. Use the same time unit for elapsed time and half-life, then calculate.

Radioactive decay formula

N = N₀ × (1/2)t/T½, where N is the amount remaining, N₀ is the initial amount, t is elapsed time and T½ is the half-life. The decay constant is λ = ln(2)/T½. The model assumes a constant probability of decay for each nucleus.

Worked examples

One half-life

A sample starts at 80 grams and has a half-life of 5 days. After 5 days, one half-life has passed and 40 grams remain.

Two half-lives

After 10 days, two half-lives have passed. Half of 40 grams remains, so the sample contains 20 grams. This is 25% of the initial amount.

Three half-lives

After 15 days, 10 grams remain. Each half-life halves the amount present at the start of that interval: 80, 40, 20, then 10 grams.

Understanding the result

The amount remaining decreases exponentially rather than by the same fixed mass each time period. After one half-life, 50% remains; after two, 25%; after three, 12.5%; and after four, 6.25%. The number of half-lives elapsed is elapsed time divided by the half-life.

Common mistakes and limitations

  • Use matching units for elapsed time and half-life. For example, do not enter elapsed time in hours and half-life in days without converting one.
  • Half-life is not the time for every nucleus in a sample to decay.
  • The model describes statistical behaviour of a large sample. Individual nuclear decay events are random.
  • This calculator does not identify an isotope or provide medical, environmental or radiation-safety advice.
  • Displayed results are rounded and may be fractional because the formula estimates an expected amount.

Frequently asked questions

Does a radioactive substance disappear after one half-life?

No. About half of the original radioactive nuclei remain after one half-life. The remaining amount continues to halve during each subsequent half-life.

Can half-life be measured in years?

Yes. Any time unit can be used as long as elapsed time and half-life use the same unit.

What is the decay constant?

It is λ = ln(2)/T½ and represents the decay probability per unit time in the exponential model.
