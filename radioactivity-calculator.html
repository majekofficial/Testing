Radioactive decay and the meaning of half-life

Radioactive decay is the spontaneous transformation of an unstable atomic nucleus into a more stable state. During decay, the nucleus may emit alpha particles, beta particles or gamma radiation, depending on the type of nuclear transformation. A radioactive sample contains many unstable nuclei, and individual decay events are random. However, the behaviour of a large sample can be described using a predictable statistical model. The Phademy Radioactive Decay Calculator uses the half-life model to estimate how much of a sample remains after a specified time.

The calculator requires an initial amount, elapsed time and half-life. It returns the remaining amount, the amount that has decayed, the percentage remaining, the number of half-lives elapsed and the decay constant. The time unit used for elapsed time must match the unit used for half-life. For example, if half-life is entered in years, elapsed time must also be entered in years.

What is radioactive decay?

An atomic nucleus contains protons and neutrons. Some combinations of these particles are unstable and may change spontaneously. The process is called radioactive decay. The original nucleus is called the parent nucleus, and the resulting nucleus is called the daughter nucleus. Depending on the decay process, particles or electromagnetic radiation may be emitted. Radioactive decay is a nuclear process, not an ordinary chemical reaction, so it is generally not changed by everyday chemical conditions such as pressure or chemical bonding.

A single unstable nucleus does not have a predictable exact decay time. It is impossible to say precisely when a particular nucleus will decay using the basic half-life model. Instead, half-life describes the statistical behaviour of a large population of identical radioactive nuclei. The model predicts the fraction expected to remain after a period, not the identity or exact decay time of each individual atom.

What half-life means

The half-life of a radioactive isotope is the time required for half of the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay. After one half-life, half the original radioactive quantity remains. After two half-lives, half of that remaining amount is left, which is one quarter of the original. After three half-lives, one eighth remains. The amount halves repeatedly; it does not decrease by the same fixed amount every time.

N = N₀ × (1/2)^(t / T½)

In this formula, N is the remaining amount, N₀ is the initial amount, t is the elapsed time and T½ is the half-life. The exponent t/T½ tells you how many half-lives have passed. If t equals one half-life, the exponent is 1 and half remains. If t equals two half-lives, the exponent is 2 and one quarter remains. If t is half a half-life, the remaining fraction is the square root of one half, approximately 0.7071.

How to use the radioactive decay calculator

  4. Make sure elapsed time and half-life use the same unit, such as both seconds, both days or both years.
  5. Calculate and inspect the remaining amount, decayed amount, percentage remaining and number of half-lives elapsed.

The amount may be expressed as grams, kilograms, number of nuclei or another consistent quantity. The calculator treats the amount as a numerical quantity and does not convert between mass and number of atoms. If the initial amount is 80 g, the result is in grams. If the initial amount is 1,000 nuclei, the result is an expected number of nuclei and may be fractional in the mathematical model.

Remaining amount and amount decayed

The remaining amount is the portion of the original radioactive sample that has not yet decayed according to the model. The amount decayed is the initial amount minus the remaining amount. If a sample begins at 80 g and 20 g remains, the amount decayed is 60 g. The two amounts should add to the initial amount, apart from small rounding differences in the displayed result.

Amount decayed = N₀ − N
Percentage remaining = (N / N₀) × 100%

The percentage remaining describes the proportion of the original radioactive quantity still present. The percentage decayed is 100% minus the percentage remaining. A sample does not become exactly zero after a fixed number of half-lives in the mathematical exponential model. It approaches zero continuously, although a real sample contains a finite number of nuclei and eventually may have very few radioactive nuclei left.

The decay constant

The decay constant, usually written λ (lambda), is the probability per unit time that an individual nucleus will decay, in the statistical model. It is related to half-life by λ = ln(2)/T½. A shorter half-life corresponds to a larger decay constant, meaning the isotope decays more rapidly. A longer half-life corresponds to a smaller decay constant and slower decay.

λ = ln(2) / T½ ≈ 0.693147 / T½

The unit of the decay constant is the reciprocal of the time unit used for half-life. If half-life is measured in seconds, λ is measured per second. If half-life is measured in years, λ is measured per year. Do not compare numerical decay constants without checking their time units.

Worked examples

Example 1: one half-life

A sample begins with 80 g and has a half-life of 5 days. After 5 days, one half-life has passed.

Remaining amount = 80 × (1/2)¹ = 40 g. The amount decayed is 80 − 40 = 40 g, so 50% remains.

Example 2: two half-lives

The same 80 g sample is observed after 10 days. Two half-lives have passed because 10/5 = 2.

Remaining amount = 80 × (1/2)² = 80/4 = 20 g. The amount decayed is 60 g, and 25% remains.

Example 3: three half-lives

A sample starts at 160 mg and has a half-life of 2 hours. After 6 hours, three half-lives have passed.

Remaining amount = 160 × (1/2)³ = 20 mg. The sample has lost 140 mg, and 12.5% remains.

Example 4: half a half-life

A sample begins at 100 units and is observed after half of one half-life. The elapsed-time ratio is 0.5.

Remaining fraction = (1/2)^0.5 = √0.5 ≈ 0.7071. The remaining amount is about 70.71 units, or 70.71% of the original.

Example 5: calculating the decay constant

An isotope has a half-life of 10 years. The decay constant is λ = ln(2)/10 ≈ 0.0693 per year.

The unit is per year because the half-life was entered in years. If the half-life were converted to days, the numerical value of λ would change to a per-day value.

Example 6: checking elapsed-time units

Suppose a half-life is 24 hours. An elapsed time of 2 days must be converted to 48 hours before using the formula.

The number of half-lives is 48/24 = 2, so one quarter of the original amount remains. Entering 2 as though it were 2 hours would produce a different and incorrect interpretation.

Why radioactive decay is exponential

Radioactive decay is exponential because the rate of decay at a given moment is proportional to the number of radioactive nuclei remaining. When many nuclei are present, more decays are expected per unit time. As the sample becomes smaller, fewer decays are expected per unit time. The decrease therefore slows in absolute amount even though the same fraction is lost during each half-life.

This differs from a linear decrease. A linear model removes the same quantity in every equal time interval. A half-life model removes half of what remains in each half-life interval. Starting with 100 units, the amounts after successive half-lives are 100, 50, 25, 12.5, 6.25 and so on. The amount removed in each interval becomes smaller: 50, 25, 12.5, 6.25 and so on.

Different isotopes have different half-lives

Radioactive isotopes can have half-lives ranging from extremely short fractions of a second to billions of years. The half-life is a characteristic property of a particular radioactive nuclide under the usual nuclear-decay model. It is not the time for every atom in a sample to disappear. A sample containing many nuclei follows the statistical pattern, while a very small number of nuclei can show substantial random variation.

The half-life value must match the isotope and decay process being studied. Do not use a half-life from a different isotope simply because the element name is similar. Isotopes of the same element have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons, and they can have very different nuclear stability and half-lives.

Radioactive decay and activity

Activity is the number of nuclear decays occurring per unit time. It is measured in becquerels (Bq), where one becquerel is one decay per second. Activity is related to the number of undecayed nuclei and the decay constant by A = λN. As the number of radioactive nuclei decreases, activity also decreases for a single isotope with a constant decay constant.

The amount of material and its activity are related but are not identical quantities. A mass measurement in grams tells you how much material is present, while activity tells you how many decays occur each second. To calculate activity from mass, additional information such as molar mass, isotopic abundance and the number of atoms is required. The Phademy calculator does not perform that conversion; it calculates the remaining numerical amount from an initial amount and half-life.

Uses of half-life in science

Half-life is used in nuclear physics, chemistry, environmental science, archaeology and medicine. Radiometric dating uses the known decay of certain isotopes to estimate the age of materials. Medical imaging and treatment may use radioactive isotopes with carefully selected properties. Environmental studies may track radioactive contaminants over time. These applications require specialised measurement, calibration, uncertainty analysis and safety controls beyond a simple half-life calculation.

Carbon-14 dating is a familiar example in which the remaining carbon-14 in once-living material is compared with a reference level to estimate age. The method relies on assumptions about the initial carbon-14 content and changes in the atmosphere over time. It is not simply a matter of entering any sample mass into a half-life calculator. Real dating work uses measured isotope ratios and calibration information.

Randomness, expected values and small samples

The exponential equation describes the expected behaviour of a large sample. It does not predict the exact number of individual nuclei that will decay during a short interval. For a sample containing an enormous number of nuclei, random variations are usually small relative to the total. For a sample containing only a few nuclei, the actual count can differ substantially from the smooth mathematical expectation.

This is why a calculated remaining amount may contain a decimal even when the sample is described as a count of atoms. The equation gives an expected quantity. A real sample cannot contain a fraction of a nucleus. In a practical interpretation, the result is an average expectation or a model estimate, and individual decay events remain discrete and random.

Common mistakes and limitations

  • Using different units for elapsed time and half-life. Convert them to the same unit first.
  • Subtracting the same fixed amount after every half-life. The amount halves repeatedly; the reduction is not constant.
  • Confusing half-life with the time for all material to disappear. The ideal model approaches zero rather than reaching it at a fixed time.
  • Treating a fractional expected number of nuclei as a literal fractional atom. It is a statistical expectation.
  • Using a half-life for the wrong isotope. Confirm the nuclide and the value supplied by a reliable source or question.
  • Confusing remaining amount with amount decayed. They add to the initial amount.
  • Assuming the calculator measures radiation. It performs a mathematical estimate and is not a detector.

This calculator is an educational exponential-decay model. It does not identify isotopes, measure radiation, estimate dose, assess contamination, model decay chains or provide radiation-safety advice. It assumes a single decay process with a constant half-life and a large-sample statistical model. Radiation protection decisions require qualified professionals, calibrated instruments and applicable safety regulations.

Practice questions with answers

  1. A 100 g sample has a half-life of 4 days. How much remains after 4 days? Answer: 50 g.
  2. How much remains after 8 days? Answer: 25 g.
  3. A 160 mg sample has a half-life of 3 hours. How much remains after 9 hours? Answer: 20 mg.
  4. What percentage remains after four half-lives? Answer: (1/2)^4 × 100 = 6.25%.
  5. A sample begins at 200 units and 50 remain. How much has decayed? Answer: 150 units.
  6. Find the decay constant for a half-life of 20 seconds. Answer: 0.693147/20 ≈ 0.0347 s⁻¹.
  7. If elapsed time is 48 hours and half-life is 2 days, how many half-lives have passed? Answer: 1, after converting 2 days to 48 hours.
  8. Does a sample become exactly zero after ten half-lives in the ideal model? Answer: No. It approaches zero; 1/1024 of the original remains.

Frequently asked questions

What is half-life?

Half-life is the time required for half of the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay. After each additional half-life, half of the remaining quantity is left.

How do I calculate radioactive decay?

Multiply the initial amount by one half raised to the power of elapsed time divided by half-life: N = N₀(1/2)^(t/T½).

What happens after two half-lives?

One quarter of the original radioactive amount remains, because half of the original remains after the first interval and half of that remains after the second.

Does radioactive material disappear after one half-life?

No. Half remains after one half-life. The quantity continues to decrease by half during each further half-life.

Why must the time units match?

The exponent compares elapsed time with half-life. Both must use the same unit so the ratio represents the correct number of half-lives.

What is the decay constant?

The decay constant is the probability rate per unit time in the exponential model. It is related to half-life by λ = ln(2)/T½.

What is the difference between activity and amount?

Amount describes how much radioactive material or how many nuclei are present. Activity describes the number of decays per second.

Can half-life be changed by temperature or pressure?

Ordinary changes in temperature and pressure do not significantly change the nuclear half-life of an isotope. Radioactive decay is a nuclear process.

Why can the remaining amount be a decimal?

The formula gives an expected quantity for a statistical sample. A real count of nuclei is a whole number, but the mathematical expectation can be fractional.

Can this calculator tell me whether an object is safe?

No. It does not measure radiation, dose or contamination. Use appropriate calibrated instruments and qualified radiation-safety guidance.

Can I use any isotope half-life?

Use the half-life for the specific isotope in the problem. Different isotopes can have very different half-lives.

What is the unit of the decay constant?

It is the reciprocal of the time unit used for half-life, such as per second, per day or per year.
