Frequency tables: organising and analysing data

A frequency table organises data by showing how often each value occurs. Instead of writing a long list of repeated observations, you list each distinct value and its frequency. This makes a data set easier to read, compare and analyse. Frequency tables are common in secondary-school statistics, classroom surveys, test-score summaries, quality checks and simple research reports. The Phademy Frequency Table Calculator works with discrete values and their matching frequencies, then calculates the total frequency, sum of fx, mean, median, mode and range.

A frequency table does more than shorten a list. It makes the number of observations visible and helps you check whether the data have been counted correctly. The frequency tells you how many times a value appears. A high frequency means that value occurs often in the data, while a frequency of zero means it does not occur. The calculator can combine repeated values entered more than once, which helps when a list has not been arranged in order.

For example, if values are 2, 3, 4 and 5 and the frequencies are 1, 2, 4 and 1, this means there is one observation of 2, two observations of 3, four observations of 4 and one observation of 5. The total number of observations is 1 + 2 + 4 + 1 = 8. The frequency list is not another set of data values; it tells you how many times each corresponding value occurs.

Understanding the parts of a frequency table

A simple discrete frequency table usually has two columns: the value x and its frequency f. The value is the observation or category being counted. The frequency is the number of observations with that value. For calculating a mean, a third column fx is often added. It is found by multiplying each value by its frequency. The sum of the frequencies is the total number of observations, and the sum of fx is the total of all observations when repeated values are expanded.

Total frequency = Σf
Sum of products = Σfx
Mean = Σfx / Σf

A frequency table can also include cumulative frequency, which is the running total of frequencies up to a value. Cumulative frequency helps answer questions such as “how many observations are at or below 4?” or “what is the position of the median?” The current calculator focuses on the core discrete measures and does not display a grouped cumulative-frequency curve.

Calculating the mean from a frequency table

The mean is the arithmetic average. When values repeat, the frequency table provides a shortcut: multiply each value by its frequency, add all the products, and divide by the total frequency. This is equivalent to adding every observation individually, but it avoids writing repeated values out many times.

x̄ = Σfx / Σf

Suppose values 1, 2 and 3 have frequencies 2, 3 and 1. The products are 2, 6 and 3, giving Σfx = 11. The total frequency is 2 + 3 + 1 = 6. The mean is 11/6 ≈ 1.833. A mean does not have to be one of the values in the table. It is a balance point of the numerical data.

Finding the median from frequency data

The median is the middle value after the observations are arranged in ascending order. If the total number of observations is odd, the median is the value at position (n + 1)/2. If the total is even, the median is the average of the values at positions n/2 and (n/2) + 1. A frequency table lets you locate these positions without expanding every repeated observation into a long list.

For example, if the values 1, 2 and 3 have frequencies 2, 3 and 1, the ordered data are 1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 3. There are six observations, so the median is the average of the third and fourth values. Both are 2, so the median is 2. In another table, the two central values may differ, in which case their average is the median.

Finding the mode and range

The mode is the value with the highest frequency. A data set may have one mode, more than one mode, or no useful mode if every value occurs equally often. A frequency table makes the most common value easy to identify. The range is the maximum value minus the minimum value. It measures the width of the data from the smallest observation to the largest, but it does not show how values are distributed between those extremes.

The mode can be useful for categorical or discrete information because it identifies the most common response. The mean is usually not meaningful for labels such as favourite colour, but the mode is. The range is simple to calculate, yet it is sensitive to extreme values. A single unusually high or low observation can make the range large even when most values are close together.

Worked examples

Example 1: a small discrete table

Values: 1, 2, 3. Frequencies: 2, 3, 1. The total frequency is 6. The products fx are 2, 6 and 3, so their sum is 11.

Mean = 11/6 ≈ 1.833. The ordered data are 1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 3, so the median is 2. The mode is 2 and the range is 3 − 1 = 2.

Example 2: test scores

Five score values are 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8 with frequencies 1, 2, 4, 2 and 1. The total frequency is 10. The sum fx is 4 + 10 + 24 + 14 + 8 = 60.

The mean is 60/10 = 6. The fifth and sixth observations are both 6, so the median is 6. The mode is 6, and the range is 8 − 4 = 4.

Example 3: repeated values in raw data

Suppose the raw list is 2, 3, 2, 4, 3, 2. The frequency table has value 2 with frequency 3, value 3 with frequency 2, and value 4 with frequency 1.

The total is 6 and the sum is 16, so the mean is 16/6 ≈ 2.667. The ordered list is 2, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4. The median is (2 + 3)/2 = 2.5, the mode is 2 and the range is 2.

Example 4: an even number of observations

Values 10, 20 and 30 have frequencies 1, 2 and 1. The ordered data are 10, 20, 20, 30.

There are four observations. The two middle values are 20 and 20, so the median is 20. If the values were 10, 20, 30 and 40, the median would be (20 + 30)/2 = 25.

Example 5: comparing two groups

Group A has values 5, 6 and 7 with frequencies 1, 4 and 1. Its mean is (5 + 24 + 7)/6 = 6. Group B has values 3, 6 and 9 with frequencies 1, 4 and 1. Its mean is also 6.

Both groups have the same mean and median, but Group B is more spread out. This shows why one summary measure is not enough to describe a data set fully.

Discrete data and grouped data are not the same

Discrete data are recorded as separate values, often whole-number counts or scores. A frequency table can list each distinct value exactly. Grouped data combine observations into intervals, such as 10–19 or 20–29. Grouping is useful when there are many possible values or a continuous measurement, but it hides the exact individual observations. Calculations from grouped data often use class midpoints and therefore estimate the mean.

Do not enter a class interval such as “10–19” as a numerical value with a frequency of 8. That interval contains several possible values, and the calculator would not know the distribution within it. For grouped data, use the midpoint of each class only when your course method explicitly calls for an estimated mean. Median and mode for grouped data also use class boundaries and interpolation formulas, which are outside this calculator’s current scope.

Why mean, median and mode can tell different stories

The mean uses every numerical value and is affected by extreme observations. The median depends on the middle position and is more resistant to unusually large or small values. The mode identifies the most frequent value. A data set of household incomes may have a high mean because a few incomes are extremely large, while the median may better describe a typical household. A shoe shop may care more about the mode because it identifies the most common shoe size sold.

Consider values 2, 3, 3, 4 and 28. The mean is 8, the median is 3 and the mode is 3. The high value 28 pulls the mean upwards, but the middle and most common values remain 3. Reporting only the mean would hide the fact that most observations are close to 3. Good statistical interpretation considers the shape and context of the data, not just the calculated answer.

Reading and checking a frequency table

Before trusting a result, verify that the frequencies add to the number of observations you expect. Check that each frequency belongs to the correct value and that no category has been omitted. If the data were collected from a class of 40 students, a table of mutually exclusive responses should normally have a total frequency of 40, unless some students did not respond or more than one response was allowed.

Look for impossible values and unusual patterns. A negative frequency is not meaningful in an ordinary count table. A decimal frequency is usually inappropriate when counting individual people or objects. A frequency of zero may be useful in a table prepared in advance, but it does not add observations to the total. If you have repeated values in the input list, the calculator combines them; check the resulting table to confirm the combined counts are what you intended.

Applications of frequency tables

Teachers use frequency tables to summarise test scores, attendance and survey responses. Businesses use them to count product sizes, customer choices, order quantities and defect types. Researchers use them to inspect the distribution of observations before choosing statistical methods. In a laboratory, a frequency table may show how often a measurement falls within a particular discrete value. In each case, the table turns a collection of observations into a structure that can be checked and interpreted.

Frequency tables also prepare students for histograms, bar charts, cumulative-frequency graphs and probability. A bar chart is suitable for discrete categories or values, while a histogram is used for continuous grouped data and the bars touch to show intervals. A cumulative-frequency graph uses running totals to show how many observations lie below a boundary. These are related ideas, but they are not interchangeable representations.

Common mistakes and limitations

  • Entering frequencies in a different order from the values. Each frequency must match the value beside it.
  • Forgetting that blank frequencies mean each listed value occurs once.
  • Entering a grouped interval as though it were an individual observation.
  • Using the mean for categories that are not numerical measurements.
  • Assuming the mode is always unique. A data set can have more than one mode.
  • Ignoring extreme values when interpreting the mean and range.
  • Treating a frequency as a percentage. Frequency is a count; relative frequency is a proportion of the total.

Practice questions with answers

  2. For the same table, calculate Σfx. Answer: 2 + 6 + 3 = 11.
  3. Find the mean of values 2 and 4 with frequencies 3 and 1. Answer: (6 + 4)/4 = 2.5.
  4. What is the mode if 5 occurs 8 times and 6 occurs 3 times? Answer: 5.
  5. Find the range of 4, 4, 7, 9 and 10. Answer: 10 − 4 = 6.
  6. For 2, 2, 3, 3, what is the median? Answer: (2 + 3)/2 = 2.5.
  7. If the frequencies are 4, 5 and 6, what is n? Answer: 15.

Frequently asked questions

What is a frequency table?

It is a table that lists each distinct value or category and the number of times it occurs in a data set.

What does frequency mean in statistics?

Frequency is the count of observations with a particular value or in a particular category.

How do I calculate the mean from a frequency table?

Multiply each value by its frequency, add the products and divide by the sum of the frequencies.

How do I find the median from a frequency table?

Find the total number of observations, locate the middle position or positions, and use the cumulative counts to identify the value at those positions.

How do I find the mode?

The mode is the value with the greatest frequency. There may be more than one mode if multiple values share the highest frequency.

What is the difference between frequency and relative frequency?

Frequency is a count. Relative frequency is the count divided by the total number of observations, often expressed as a fraction, decimal or percentage.

Can I leave the frequency field blank?

Yes. A blank frequency field means each value you enter is counted once.

Can I enter repeated values?

Yes. The calculator combines repeated values into a single row and adds their frequencies.

Can this calculator handle grouped data?

No. It is designed for discrete values. Grouped intervals require class boundaries and methods that are not part of this version.

Why does the mean differ from the median?

The mean uses every value and can be pulled by extreme observations. The median is based on the middle position and is less affected by extremes.

Can a data set have no mode?

If every value occurs equally often, there may be no single most frequent value. Some data sets have multiple modes.

What should the total frequency equal?

It should equal the number of observations represented by the table. If it does not, check for missing values, duplicate counts or non-response.
