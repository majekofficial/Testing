Estimating daily calorie needs

Your body burns energy even when you are resting, and more when you move. This calculator estimates both. Enter your sex, age, weight, height and activity level, in metric or US units, and it returns the calories you need each day to maintain your weight, your resting metabolism (BMR) and a range of daily figures for slow weight loss or gain.

How the estimate is built

It uses the Mifflin–St Jeor equation, a widely used formula for resting energy expenditure:

Men: BMR = 10 × kg + 6.25 × cm − 5 × age + 5

Women: BMR = 10 × kg + 6.25 × cm − 5 × age − 161

Maintenance calories are the BMR multiplied by an activity factor: 1.2 for sedentary, 1.375 for light activity (exercise 1–3 days a week), 1.55 for moderate (3–5 days), 1.725 for very active (6–7 days) and 1.9 for extra active, such as hard training or a physical job.

Worked examples

A moderately active man

Age 30, 70 kg, 175 cm. BMR = 700 + 1,093.75 − 150 + 5 = 1,648.75, shown as about 1,650. With moderate activity, 1,648.75 × 1.55 ≈ 2,556, which the calculator rounds to 2,560 calories a day.

A lightly active woman

Age 28, 60 kg, 165 cm. BMR = 600 + 1,031.25 − 140 − 161 = 1,330.25, about 1,330. With light activity, 1,330.25 × 1.375 ≈ 1,829, shown as 1,830.

Different activity levels for the same man

For the 30-year-old man above, the maintenance figure is about 1,980 if sedentary, 2,270 if lightly active, 2,560 if moderately active, 2,840 if very active and 3,130 if extra active. Choosing the wrong level is the most common source of error.

US units

Choose US units to enter pounds, feet and inches. A 40-year-old man who weighs 180 lb and is 5 ft 10 in tall, and is sedentary, has a BMR of about 1,730 and a maintenance figure of about 2,080.

Using the goal table

Under Show workings the calculator lists daily figures for maintaining, losing or gaining a small amount per week. For the 30-year-old man above, that is 2,280 calories a day to lose 0.25 kg a week, 2,010 to lose 0.5 kg and 2,830 to gain 0.25 kg. These are rough planning numbers. A typical rule of thumb is that about 7,700 calories equal a kilogram of body weight, but real changes vary with water, muscle and metabolism.

Staying realistic and safe

  • The figures are estimates with a margin of error of perhaps 10% either way. Track your weight over several weeks and adjust, instead of treating the result as exact.
  • The calculator is a guide, not medical advice. Do not use it to justify eating far below your needs.
  • People who are pregnant, breastfeeding, under 18, living with a medical condition or recovering from an eating disorder need individual advice from a doctor or registered dietitian.
  • Food quality, sleep and strength training matter as much as the total number of calories.

Common mistakes

  • Overestimating activity. Most desk-based people with a few workouts a week sit nearer “light” than “very active”.
  • Entering weight in pounds while Metric is selected.
  • Treating the BMR as a daily target. It is the minimum your body burns at rest, not what you should eat.
  • Expecting results from a short-term change. Estimates need weeks of tracking.

Your height and weight also give a body mass index: try the BMI Calculator. If you need to check food or money in a weekly plan, the Percentage Calculator and Basic Calculator help with the arithmetic.

Frequently asked questions

What is BMR?

Basal metabolic rate is the energy your body uses at rest to keep you alive: breathing, circulation and cell repair.

How accurate is the calculator?

It is an estimate. Individuals can differ from the formula by about 10%, so use it as a starting point and adjust based on real results.

Why does it ask for sex?

The Mifflin–St Jeor formula uses a different constant for men and women, which reflects average differences in body composition.

Should I eat the lowest figure shown?

Not automatically. Slow, sustainable changes are usually safer, and anyone with a medical condition should speak to a professional first.
