Rearranging a formula to isolate the letter you need

The subject of a formula is the letter on its own on one side, such as v in v = u + at. Changing the subject means rearranging the formula so a different letter stands alone. Type the formula, choose the letter from the list that appears and the calculator shows the rearranged version with its steps.

The principle

Treat the formula like an equation: do the same thing to both sides, undoing operations in the reverse order from which they were applied. If t is multiplied by a and then added to u, subtract u first, then divide by a.

Worked examples

A formula from motion

v = u + at. To make t the subject, subtract u: v − u = at, then divide by a: t = (v − u) ÷ a. Choosing a gives a = (v − u) ÷ t, and choosing u gives u = v − at.

A longer formula

s = ut + ½at². Choosing u: subtract ½at² and divide by t to get u = (2s − at²) ÷ 2t. The calculator clears the fraction first by multiplying by 2.

A square root appears

A = πr². To make r the subject, divide by π and take the square root: r = ±√(A ÷ π). The ± sign is shown because both a positive and a negative number give the same square. For a physical length you take the positive value.

Temperature conversion

F = 9C/5 + 32. Subtract 32 and multiply by 5/9 to make C the subject: C = 5(F − 32) ÷ 9, shown by the calculator as (5F − 160) ÷ 9.

Letters in a denominator

1/f = 1/u + 1/v is the lens formula. Choosing v gives v = −fu ÷ (f − u), which is the same as fu ÷ (u − f). Rearranging fractions like this is a common source of mistakes by hand.

Limits

  • Letters that appear raised to the first or second power can be isolated. For a cubic, such as V = (4/3)πr³ solved for r, the calculator shows a dash because cube roots are not supported here.
  • Write products explicitly if needed, for example v = u + a*t, and use ^ for powers.
  • Choose the letter in the list that appears below the formula box. It lists only the letters found in your formula.

Common mistakes

  • Moving a term without changing its sign.
  • Dividing only part of one side. Divide the whole side, using brackets when it has several terms.
  • Forgetting the ± when taking a square root.
  • Cancelling terms across a sum, such as treating (a + b) ÷ a as 1 + b.

Frequently asked questions

What does “make x the subject” mean?

Rearrange the formula so x is alone on one side and everything else is on the other.

Why does the answer include ±?

A square root has two possible values. Choose the positive or negative one that fits the problem.

How do I choose the subject?

After you type the formula, a list of the letters it contains appears. Choose the one you want to isolate.

Can I rearrange any formula?

Most school formulas work. Formulas that need cube roots or higher-order solutions may show a dash.
