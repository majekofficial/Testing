Understanding right-triangle trigonometry

Trigonometry describes relationships between angles and side lengths. It is used in school mathematics, technical drawing, construction, navigation, surveying and introductory physics. This Phademy Trigonometry Calculator focuses on right-angled triangles: triangles containing one angle of exactly 90 degrees. It calculates sine, cosine and tangent for an acute angle, and it can complete a right triangle when you provide enough valid information. The aim is not only to return a number. The working helps you see which sides are being compared and why a particular ratio is appropriate.

A right triangle has three sides. The hypotenuse is opposite the right angle and is always the longest side. The other two sides are called the opposite and adjacent sides relative to the acute angle you are using. These names are not permanent labels for the triangle: if you choose the other acute angle, the opposite and adjacent sides exchange roles. The hypotenuse does not change.

What the trigonometry calculator can find

The calculator has two closely related uses. First, it evaluates the three basic trigonometric ratios for an angle entered in degrees or radians. Second, it solves a right triangle from an acute angle and one side, or from two valid side lengths. From a completed triangle, you can read the missing side, the other acute angle and the ratios. The tool checks that the information is consistent with a right triangle instead of accepting impossible side combinations.

  • Sine (sin): opposite side divided by hypotenuse.
  • Cosine (cos): adjacent side divided by hypotenuse.
  • Tangent (tan): opposite side divided by adjacent side.
  • Inverse sine, cosine or tangent: used to recover an angle from a known ratio.
  • Pythagoras’ theorem: used to relate the three side lengths.

How to use the calculator correctly

  1. For ratios only, enter an acute angle and choose whether the value is in degrees or radians.
  2. For a triangle with a known angle and one side, enter the angle, enter the side length, and identify it as opposite, adjacent or hypotenuse.
  3. For a triangle with two known sides, enter the two side lengths in their matching fields. The calculator uses the right-triangle relationship to find the third side and the acute angles.
  4. Read the result and the working. Check the side labels against the angle shown in the diagram before copying the answer into an assignment.

Use one method at a time. Do not combine the single-known-side field with all three side fields, because that would give the calculator competing descriptions of the same triangle. Side lengths must be positive. The hypotenuse must be longer than either shorter side, and two shorter sides must satisfy Pythagoras’ theorem.

SOH CAH TOA explained

SOH CAH TOA is a memory aid for the three main ratios. SOH means Sine = Opposite / Hypotenuse. CAH means Cosine = Adjacent / Hypotenuse. TOA means Tangent = Opposite / Adjacent. The letters are useful only after you have correctly identified the angle and labelled its sides. A common mistake is to memorise the letters but select the wrong side. Always begin by marking the angle you are working with.

A ratio compares two lengths, so the units cancel. If the opposite side is 3 cm and the hypotenuse is 5 cm, sine is 3/5 or 0.6. It is not 0.6 cm. This is why sine, cosine and tangent are dimensionless numbers. For an acute angle, sine and cosine lie between 0 and 1. Tangent is positive and can be greater than 1 when the opposite side is longer than the adjacent side.

sin θ = opposite / hypotenuse
cos θ = adjacent / hypotenuse
tan θ = opposite / adjacent

Pythagoras’ theorem and triangle completion

Pythagoras’ theorem states that the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the two shorter sides. If the opposite side is a, the adjacent side is b and the hypotenuse is c, then a² + b² = c². To find the hypotenuse, take the square root of a² + b². To find one shorter side, subtract the square of the other shorter side from c² and take the square root. The subtraction is valid only when the hypotenuse is longer than the known shorter side.

The theorem is a useful independent check on a trigonometric answer. If a triangle has shorter sides 5 and 12, the hypotenuse is 13 because 5² + 12² = 25 + 144 = 169, and √169 = 13. If your calculator gives a different result for those exact inputs, check the entered fields and units.

Inverse trigonometric functions: finding an angle

When the side ratio is known and the angle is unknown, use an inverse trigonometric function. The notation sin⁻¹(x), cos⁻¹(x) and tan⁻¹(x) means inverse sine, inverse cosine and inverse tangent. It does not mean 1 divided by sine, cosine or tangent. For example, if opposite/hypotenuse = 0.5, then θ = sin⁻¹(0.5) = 30° in degree mode. The calculator uses these inverse relationships when it completes a triangle from side lengths.

The inverse function returns an angle in the selected unit. A result of 0.5236 in radian mode is approximately 30 degrees, not 0.5236 degrees. If an answer seems unfamiliar, check the degree/radian setting before assuming the calculation is wrong.

Degrees and radians

A degree divides a full rotation into 360 equal parts. A radian measures an angle using the radius of a circle. One full turn is 2π radians, a straight angle is π radians, and a right angle is π/2 radians. The conversion is radians = degrees × π/180, while degrees = radians × 180/π. Thus 90° equals π/2 radians and 180° equals π radians.

Degree mode is common in school geometry and many practical drawings. Radians are common in advanced mathematics, calculus and scientific computing. The same physical angle has different numerical values in the two systems. A calculator cannot infer which unit you intended, so the unit selector matters. Never enter 30 while the tool is set to radians if your intention is 30 degrees.

Worked examples with full reasoning

Example 1: calculate the three ratios at 30 degrees

For θ = 30°, the exact sine is 1/2 = 0.5. The cosine is √3/2 ≈ 0.8660. The tangent is 1/√3 ≈ 0.5774.

These values are ratios, not lengths. They are useful checks when solving textbook questions involving the special angles 30°, 45° and 60°.

Example 2: find two sides from an angle and hypotenuse

A right triangle has a 30° angle and a hypotenuse of 10 cm. The opposite side is 10 × sin 30° = 10 × 0.5 = 5 cm.

The adjacent side is 10 × cos 30° = 10 × 0.8660 ≈ 8.66 cm. The hypotenuse remains the longest side, which is consistent with the result.

Example 3: find the hypotenuse from two shorter sides

A right triangle has perpendicular sides of 9 m and 12 m. The hypotenuse is √(9² + 12²) = √(81 + 144) = √225 = 15 m.

The 9:12:15 triangle is a scaled 3:4:5 triangle. Dividing each side by 3 gives 3, 4 and 5, which provides a quick reasonableness check.

Example 4: find an angle from two sides

Suppose the opposite side is 7 cm and the hypotenuse is 14 cm. The sine ratio is 7/14 = 0.5. Therefore θ = sin⁻¹(0.5) = 30°.

The result is the acute angle opposite the 7 cm side. If you were finding the other acute angle, it would be 60°, because the two acute angles in a right triangle add to 90°.

Example 5: use tangent when opposite and adjacent are known

A ramp rises 1.2 m over a horizontal distance of 4.0 m. The angle of elevation θ satisfies tan θ = 1.2/4.0 = 0.3.

Therefore θ = tan⁻¹(0.3) ≈ 16.7°. The ramp length is not the horizontal distance; it is the hypotenuse. Keeping those distances separate prevents a common setup error.

Special angles worth remembering

Some angles have exact trigonometric values that appear repeatedly in school exercises. At 30°, sine is 1/2, cosine is √3/2 and tangent is √3/3. At 45°, sine and cosine are both √2/2, while tangent is 1. At 60°, sine is √3/2, cosine is 1/2 and tangent is √3. These values follow from the geometry of equilateral and isosceles right triangles.

For a 45°-45°-90° triangle, the two shorter sides are equal and the hypotenuse is √2 times either shorter side. For a 30°-60°-90° triangle, the sides opposite 30°, 60° and 90° are in the ratio 1 : √3 : 2. Knowing these patterns can make exact answers easier to recognise and can help you check a decimal result.

How trigonometry appears in practical problems

Trigonometry is used whenever a right triangle represents a height, distance or direction. A surveyor may measure an angle of elevation from a known horizontal distance to estimate the height of a building. A carpenter may use a diagonal brace to check a frame. A physics student may split a force into horizontal and vertical components. In each case, the triangle is a model of the situation, and the meaning of each side must be decided before selecting a ratio.

In navigation and mapping, angles help describe direction and displacement. In construction, a roof pitch can be expressed as a rise relative to a horizontal run. In optics, a ray diagram uses angles to describe how light travels. In computer graphics, sine and cosine help describe circular motion and rotations. The right-triangle ratios are the starting point for these wider applications, although more advanced problems may require unit-circle trigonometry or vector methods.

Choosing the right ratio

Choose sine when the problem connects the opposite side and hypotenuse. Choose cosine when it connects the adjacent side and hypotenuse. Choose tangent when it connects the opposite and adjacent sides. Do not choose a ratio simply because it is the first one you remember. Draw or mark the triangle, identify the reference angle, label the sides, and then compare the two sides involved in the question.

  • If the hypotenuse is involved and the opposite side is involved, use sine.
  • If the hypotenuse is involved and the adjacent side is involved, use cosine.
  • If the opposite and adjacent sides are involved, use tangent.
  • If all three sides are involved, Pythagoras’ theorem provides an additional relationship.
  • If the angle is unknown, use the appropriate inverse ratio after forming the side fraction.

Common errors and how to avoid them

  • Mixing up opposite and adjacent: these depend on the angle selected. Mark the angle first.
  • Using the wrong angle mode: 30 degrees and 30 radians are very different values.
  • Treating a ratio as a length: sine, cosine and tangent have no length unit.
  • Calling a shorter side the hypotenuse: the hypotenuse is always opposite the right angle.
  • Rounding too early: retain extra decimal places during the working and round the final answer.
  • Using the right-triangle calculator for an oblique triangle: this tool does not solve every triangle shape.
  • Entering inconsistent side lengths: check Pythagoras before interpreting a rejected triangle as a software problem.

Limitations and responsible interpretation

This calculator is designed for right-angled triangles and acute interior angles. It does not solve arbitrary triangles using the sine rule or cosine rule. It assumes the measurements supplied are accurate and that the triangle is genuinely right-angled. Real measurements may include uncertainty, so a result displayed to many decimal places should not be mistaken for a measurement of equal precision. The tool is intended for learning and checking work; follow the precision and rounding instructions given in your class, examination or technical specification.

The diagram is a guide to the side labels and relationships, not a scale drawing. A triangle may look different on screen because the drawing is illustrative. Use the calculated values rather than estimating lengths from the picture. If the problem involves a physical structure, a safety-critical measurement or a real construction decision, use verified measurements and appropriate professional checks rather than relying on a school-level calculator alone.

Practice questions with answers

  1. A right triangle has opposite side 6 and hypotenuse 10. What is sine? Answer: 6/10 = 0.6.
  2. A triangle has adjacent side 8 and hypotenuse 10. What is cosine? Answer: 8/10 = 0.8.
  3. If opposite = 5 and adjacent = 5, what is the acute angle? Answer: tan θ = 1, so θ = 45°.
  4. Convert 60° to radians. Answer: 60 × π/180 = π/3 radians.
  5. A 13 cm hypotenuse and a 5 cm shorter side are given. Find the other shorter side. Answer: √(13² − 5²) = √144 = 12 cm.
  6. A 2 m vertical rise occurs over a 5 m horizontal run. Find the angle of elevation. Answer: tan⁻¹(2/5) ≈ 21.8°.
  7. What is cos 60°? Answer: 0.5.
  8. What is the hypotenuse of a triangle with shorter sides 7 and 24? Answer: 25, because √(49 + 576) = 25.

Frequently asked questions

What is trigonometry used for?

Trigonometry relates angles to side lengths. It is used in geometry, surveying, construction, navigation, physics, engineering and many forms of measurement.

What does SOH CAH TOA stand for?

It stands for Sine = Opposite/Hypotenuse, Cosine = Adjacent/Hypotenuse and Tangent = Opposite/Adjacent. The side labels are always relative to the angle being used.

Can this calculator find a missing side?

Yes. Enter an acute angle and one known side, and identify the side correctly. It can also complete a right triangle when two valid side lengths are supplied.

Can I find an angle if I know two sides?

Yes. The calculator uses inverse sine, inverse cosine or inverse tangent as appropriate. The angle unit setting determines whether the result is shown in degrees or radians.

Why is my sine result not in centimetres?

Sine is a ratio of two lengths. The units cancel during division, so the answer has no unit. A side length calculated from the ratio will have the same unit as the side you entered.

What is the difference between sin and sin inverse?

Sine takes an angle and returns a ratio. Inverse sine takes a valid ratio and returns an angle. The notation sin⁻¹ does not mean one divided by sin.

Why is the hypotenuse always the longest side?

It is opposite the 90-degree angle. Pythagoras’ theorem makes its square equal to the sum of the squares of the other two sides, so it must be longer than either one individually.

When should I use radians instead of degrees?

Use the unit specified by the question. Degrees are common in school geometry, while radians are common in calculus and many advanced mathematics and physics topics.

Can the calculator solve a triangle with no right angle?

No. It is designed for right-angled triangles. An oblique triangle may require the sine rule, cosine rule or another method.

Why does the calculator reject my side lengths?

The values may be zero, negative or inconsistent with a right triangle. The hypotenuse must be the longest side, and all three sides must satisfy Pythagoras’ theorem.

Should I round every step?

No. Keep extra digits during intermediate calculations and round only the final answer. Follow any specific rounding instruction in your assignment or examination.

Is the triangle diagram drawn to scale?

No. It is a labelled learning aid. Use the numerical results and formulas rather than measuring the diagram on your screen.
