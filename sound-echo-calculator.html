Calculate distance, speed or echo time

An echo is a reflected sound heard after the original sound. Measuring the time between making a sound and hearing its echo can help estimate the distance to a reflecting surface. This calculator uses the speed-distance-time relationship and accounts for the sound travelling to the surface and back.

How to use it

  1. Choose whether you want to find distance, round-trip echo time or sound speed.
  2. Enter the two other known quantities.
  3. Calculate to see the result and formula used.

Formula for an echo

For an echo, the measured time is the total time for the sound to travel to the reflecting surface and return. Therefore, distance to the surface d = (v × t)/2. Echo time t = 2d/v. Sound speed v = 2d/t. The factor of two is essential because the sound covers twice the one-way distance.

Worked example

A person shouts towards a cliff and hears the echo 2 seconds later. If the speed of sound is 343 m/s, the distance to the cliff is (343 × 2) ÷ 2 = 343 metres. The sound travelled approximately 686 metres in total, out and back.

Speed of sound in air

The speed of sound in air depends mainly on temperature and also on the medium. Around room temperature, 343 metres per second is a commonly used approximate value. In colder air the speed is lower, while in warmer air it is higher. Sound travels at different speeds in liquids and solids, so do not use the air value for another material.

Common mistakes and limitations

  • Enter the total round-trip echo time, not half the time.
  • Enter the one-way distance to the reflecting surface.
  • Use consistent SI units: metres, seconds and metres per second.
  • Background noise, wind, temperature and the quality of the reflecting surface can affect a real measurement.
  • The tool assumes the sound travels at a constant speed along the path and that the echo returns along the same route.

Frequently asked questions

Why do I divide by two when finding distance?

The sound travels from the source to the object and then back to the listener. The measured echo time covers both journeys.

Is 343 m/s always the speed of sound?

No. It is an approximate value for air near room temperature. The actual speed changes with temperature and the medium.

Can this calculate underwater sonar distance?

The same round-trip principle applies, but you must enter the appropriate speed of sound in water rather than the speed in air.
