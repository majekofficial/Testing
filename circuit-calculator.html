Electric circuits: voltage, current, resistance and power

An electric circuit is a connected path through which electric charge can move. A simple direct-current (DC) circuit may contain a power source, conducting wires and a resistor or another electrical component. Four quantities are especially important when studying a basic resistor circuit: voltage, current, resistance and power. The Phademy Electric Circuit Calculator uses Ohm’s law and the electrical power relationship to calculate these quantities when two compatible known values are supplied.

The tool is intended for introductory school physics and simple ideal DC relationships. It is not a circuit simulator. It does not analyse a network of many components, alternating-current impedance, changing signals, transistor behaviour or the internal characteristics of a real power supply. Understanding the assumptions helps you choose the right method and interpret the result correctly.

The four main electrical quantities

Voltage

Voltage is the electrical potential difference between two points. It describes the energy transferred per unit charge as charge moves between those points. Voltage is measured in volts (V). A battery provides a potential difference that can drive current around a closed circuit. Voltage is not the same as current: voltage is a potential difference, while current is the rate of flow of charge.

Current

Electric current is the rate at which electric charge passes a point in a circuit. It is measured in amperes (A). One ampere is one coulomb of charge passing a point per second. Conventional current is defined as the direction positive charge would move, from higher potential to lower potential through the external circuit. In metallic wires, electrons move in the opposite direction to conventional current.

Resistance

Resistance describes how strongly a component opposes current for a given voltage. It is measured in ohms (Ω). For an ideal ohmic resistor at constant physical conditions, voltage is proportional to current and the ratio V/I is constant. Real components may behave differently when temperature, voltage or operating conditions change.

Electrical power

Electrical power is the rate at which electrical energy is transferred or converted. It is measured in watts (W). A resistor converts electrical energy mainly into thermal energy. A motor converts electrical energy into mechanical energy and heat. The power relationship helps estimate how quickly energy is transferred in a circuit.

Ohm’s law

Ohm’s law states that the potential difference across an ohmic conductor is equal to the current through it multiplied by its resistance, provided the physical conditions remain constant. The relationship is often written V = IR. If voltage and resistance are known, current is V/R. If voltage and current are known, resistance is V/I. If current and resistance are known, voltage is IR.

V = IR
I = V / R
R = V / I

The law is a model for ohmic behaviour. A fixed resistor at a stable temperature is a common example. A filament lamp, diode or other non-ohmic component may not have a constant resistance across all operating conditions. Do not assume Ohm’s law describes every electrical device in every situation.

Electrical power formulas

For a DC circuit, power can be calculated from voltage and current using P = VI. Combining this with Ohm’s law gives two other useful forms: P = I²R and P = V²/R. Which formula is most convenient depends on the values known. The Phademy calculator accepts exactly two known positive values among voltage, current, resistance and power, then uses the compatible relationships to calculate the remaining quantities.

P = VI
P = I²R
P = V² / R

The formulas are equivalent when the component follows the assumed ideal resistive relationship. They should not be mixed with arbitrary AC quantities without considering phase and impedance. In an AC circuit, real power may depend on the power factor, and the simple DC calculation may not represent the full situation.

Worked examples

Example 1: current from voltage and resistance

A 12 V supply is connected across a 4 Ω resistor. Using I = V/R, current = 12/4 = 3 A.

Power = VI = 12 × 3 = 36 W. The resistor converts electrical energy at a rate of 36 joules per second under the ideal assumptions.

Example 2: voltage from current and resistance

A current of 0.5 A flows through a 20 Ω resistor. Voltage = IR = 0.5 × 20 = 10 V.

Power = I²R = 0.5² × 20 = 5 W. The result is consistent with P = VI = 10 × 0.5 = 5 W.

Example 3: resistance from voltage and power

A device operates at 24 V and uses 48 W of power. From P = V²/R, resistance = V²/P = 24²/48 = 12 Ω.

The current is P/V = 48/24 = 2 A. Checking Ohm’s law gives V/I = 24/2 = 12 Ω.

Example 4: current from power and resistance

A resistor has a power dissipation of 18 W and a resistance of 2 Ω. From P = I²R, I = √(P/R) = √(18/2) = 3 A.

The voltage is IR = 3 × 2 = 6 V. The power check is VI = 6 × 3 = 18 W.

Example 5: calculate power

A 10 Ω resistor carries 2 A. Power = I²R = 2² × 10 = 40 W. The voltage across it is IR = 20 V, and P = VI = 20 × 2 = 40 W.

Series circuits

In a simple series circuit, components are connected one after another in a single path. The same current flows through every component because charge has only one route through the circuit. The supply voltage is shared between the components, and the total resistance of ideal resistors in series is the sum of their resistances.

Rₜ = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + …

For example, 2 Ω, 3 Ω and 5 Ω resistors in series have a total resistance of 10 Ω. With a 20 V supply, the circuit current is 20/10 = 2 A. The voltage drops are 4 V, 6 V and 10 V respectively. Their sum is 20 V, equal to the supply voltage. The current is the same through all three resistors.

Parallel circuits

In a parallel circuit, components are connected across the same two nodes, creating separate branches. Each branch has the same voltage as the source, while the total current is the sum of the branch currents. The equivalent resistance of ideal resistors in parallel is found by adding the reciprocals of the resistances and taking the reciprocal of the result.

1/Rₜ = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ + …

For two resistors in parallel, the equivalent resistance is Rₜ = (R₁R₂)/(R₁ + R₂). Two 6 Ω resistors in parallel have an equivalent resistance of 3 Ω. This is lower than either individual resistance because adding a branch provides another path for current. The current from the source increases for the same supply voltage.

Electrical energy and power ratings

Power describes the rate of energy transfer, while energy is the total amount transferred over time. Electrical energy can be calculated as E = Pt when power is constant. In SI units, one watt is one joule per second. A 40 W lamp operating for 5 seconds transfers 200 J of energy. In household billing, electrical energy is often measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh), where one kilowatt-hour is 3.6 million joules.

A component’s power rating indicates the power it is designed to handle under specified conditions. A resistor rated at 0.25 W should not normally be expected to dissipate 5 W continuously. Excess power can cause overheating or failure. The calculator can determine a theoretical power value, but it does not check whether a physical component is safely rated for that value.

Open circuits, short circuits and practical safety

An open circuit has a break in the conducting path, so current cannot flow through that path. A short circuit is an unintended low-resistance connection that can allow a very large current. Real power supplies and circuits may include protective devices such as fuses and circuit breakers. Never create a short circuit to test a calculation. Electrical work should be carried out with suitable training, isolation and safety procedures.

The ideal equations do not replace electrical safety rules. Mains electricity can cause serious injury or death. Do not use a calculator result as permission to touch, modify or test live wiring. For household installations, repairs and high-energy systems, use a qualified electrician and follow applicable standards.

When Ohm’s law is not enough

Complex circuits may require Kirchhoff’s current and voltage laws, network reduction, mesh analysis or nodal analysis. Alternating-current circuits may include capacitors and inductors, whose opposition to current depends on frequency and phase. Semiconductor devices may have nonlinear current-voltage relationships. Batteries may have internal resistance, and a real supply voltage can change under load. These cases require more detailed models than the four-variable ideal DC calculator.

The distinction matters because a calculator can return a mathematically valid answer from a simplified formula while the physical system behaves differently. Always identify the component and circuit assumptions in the question. If the question says “ideal resistor” or gives a simple DC circuit, Ohm’s law is likely appropriate. If it describes a diode, AC impedance, a multi-loop network or changing conditions, use the method taught for that topic.

Common mistakes and limitations

  • Mixing milliamperes with amperes without converting. 500 mA is 0.5 A.
  • Using kilohms as though they were ohms. 2 kΩ is 2,000 Ω.
  • Confusing voltage with current. They are different physical quantities with different units.
  • Using P = VI with values that do not describe the same component or operating condition.
  • Entering more than two known values when the calculator expects exactly two.
  • Applying the ideal DC relationships directly to complex AC circuits without power factor or impedance.
  • Assuming a calculated power value means the component is safe at that power. Check the component rating.

The calculator assumes an ideal DC resistive relationship and positive inputs. It does not model AC impedance, phase angle, power factor, internal resistance, temperature-dependent resistance, nonlinear components or multi-branch circuit topology. The results are educational calculations, not a design approval or electrical safety assessment.

Frequently asked questions

What is Ohm’s law?

Ohm’s law states that voltage equals current multiplied by resistance for an ohmic conductor under constant physical conditions: V = IR.

How do I calculate current?

Divide voltage by resistance. Current is measured in amperes, and the relationship is I = V/R.

How do I calculate resistance?

Divide voltage by current when both are known and the circuit follows Ohm’s law: R = V/I.

How do I calculate electrical power?

For a simple DC circuit, multiply voltage by current. You can also use P = I²R or P = V²/R when the corresponding values are known.

What is the difference between voltage and current?

Voltage is electrical potential difference, measured in volts. Current is the rate of flow of charge, measured in amperes.

What does resistance measure?

Resistance describes how strongly a component opposes current for a given voltage. It is measured in ohms.

What does the calculator expect me to enter?

Enter exactly two known positive values among voltage, current, resistance and power. Leave the other two fields blank.

Can I use the calculator for AC circuits?

The simple equations are intended for ideal DC resistor relationships. AC circuits may require impedance, phase and power-factor calculations.

What happens to current in a series circuit?

The same current flows through every component in a single-path series circuit.

What happens to voltage in a parallel circuit?

Each branch in an ideal parallel circuit has the same voltage across it.

Why can a real resistor get hot?

It converts electrical energy into thermal energy. Its temperature can affect its resistance, so real behaviour may differ from a simplified calculation.

Is a calculator result enough to choose a resistor?

No. You must also check component power rating, tolerance, temperature, voltage rating and circuit conditions. Use proper design checks and safety guidance.
